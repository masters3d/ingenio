# High Agency & Bias Towards Action in Engineering Excellence

## Overview

This specification explores how **high agency** (strong belief in one's ability to shape outcomes) and **bias towards action** (preference for doing over deliberating) combine to create exceptional engineering excellence and career success.

**Core Insight**: Both concepts share the same linguistic root in Latin _agere_ ("to do, to act") - they are complementary expressions of the same fundamental drive toward autonomous, proactive behavior.

- **High Agency**: Provides the **belief** that you can make a difference
- **Bias Towards Action**: Provides the **behavior** of actually doing it quickly
- **Combined**: "I can shape outcomes (agency) and I'll start now rather than wait (action bias)"

## Key Concepts

### High Agency
- **Definition**: Strong belief and demonstrated capability to shape outcomes through autonomous choice and action
- **Characteristics**: Proactivity, ownership, resourcefulness, initiative
- **Engineering Impact**: Engineers with high agency don't wait for perfect conditions or permission—they actively create opportunities and drive change

### Bias Towards Action
- **Definition**: Deliberate preference for taking concrete steps forward rather than extensive deliberation
- **Characteristics**: Rapid iteration, experimentation, learning through doing
- **Engineering Impact**: Fast action-feedback cycles lead to faster learning and better outcomes than prolonged planning

### The Knowledge-Action Gap
The persistent challenge in engineering: the gap between knowing what needs to be done and actually doing it. This manifests as:
- Analysis paralysis (endless planning without execution)
- Permission seeking (waiting for approval on decisions within your control)
- Perfect information fallacy (delaying until all information is available)
- Responsibility diffusion (waiting for someone else to take initiative)

### PIE Theory Connection
- **Performance**: Action bias accelerates delivery and iteration

- **Exposure**: Proactive initiatives create natural visibility

## The Action Decision Framework

### When to Act Quickly (High Action Bias)
**Two-Way Door Decisions** - Reversible, low risk:
- Reversible decisions
- Low risk of failure
- High learning value
- Small scope
- Time sensitive
- Within your authority
- Iterable improvements

**Examples**: Code refactoring, prototyping, bug fixes, documentation improvements, tool experimentation

**Principle**: "If the decision is reversible and within my scope, I should act now and iterate rather than deliberate extensively."

### When to Deliberate (Moderate Action Bias)
**Partially Reversible Decisions** - Moderate risk:
- Affects multiple teams
- Requires coordination
- Moderate resource investment
- Learning opportunity exists

**Examples**: API design, team process changes, new dependencies, architectural decisions

**Principle**: "I'll create a lightweight proposal, get quick feedback, and refine through fast iterations."

### When to Plan Carefully (Low Action Bias)
**One-Way Door Decisions** - Irreversible, high risk:
- Irreversible or very difficult to undo
- High risk with severe consequences
- Broad organizational impact
- Regulatory/legal implications
- Large resource investment

**Examples**: Data migrations, platform changes, public API commitments, security architecture, org structure changes

**Principle**: "This decision has high stakes and can't be easily reversed, so I'll invest in thorough analysis and broad consensus."

## High Agency by IC Level

### Level 0-1: Apprentice & Component Creator
**Act Quickly On**:
- Personal learning and skill development
- Code quality (tests, documentation)
- Small improvements and bug fixes
- Clarifying questions when requirements unclear

**Deliberate On**:
- Architecture changes
- Breaking API changes
- New technology choices
- Team process modifications

**Development Focus**: Build confidence through small wins, learn to distinguish reversible from irreversible decisions.

### Level 2-3: Component Designer & System Lifecycle Guide
**Act Quickly On**:
- System design within your domain
- Technical debt in your area
- Team practice improvements
- Mentoring junior engineers
- Design documents and prototypes

**Deliberate On**:
- Cross-team API changes
- Data model migrations
- Large technology migrations
- Team structure changes

**Development Focus**: Expand sphere of independent action to system-level, build relationships for faster collaboration.

### Level 4-7+: System Maintainer & Multi-System Architect
**Act Quickly On**:
- Technical strategy and direction
- Cross-system integration
- Engineering culture evolution
- Technical standards
- Platform improvements
- Industry thought leadership

**Deliberate On**:
- Company-wide technical strategy
- Major multi-quarter migrations
- Organizational structure changes
- Large budget/resource decisions

**Development Focus**: Exercise agency at organizational scale while building consensus appropriately.

## Practical Behaviors

### Individual Practices

**Morning Question: "What Can I Drive Today?"**
- What outcomes can I proactively influence?
- What problems can I solve without waiting for permission?
- What can I learn by doing rather than planning?

**The 15-Minute Rule**:
If you can start making progress in 15 minutes or less, start immediately.

**Action-Learning Cycle**:
1. Act: Take smallest meaningful step
2. Observe: What happened? What did you learn?
3. Adapt: Adjust based on feedback
4. Repeat: Iterate rapidly

**Personal Permission Slips**:
- "I don't need permission to write good code"
- "I don't need permission to fix bugs I find"
- "I don't need permission to help a teammate"
- "I don't need permission to improve documentation"

### Team Practices

**Default to Action Culture**:
- Prototype before debate
- Time-box decisions (30 minutes max)
- Document while doing
- Celebrate experiments (success or failure)

**Reduce Permission Friction**:
- Clear decision authority matrix
- Small PRs can be self-merged
- Design docs only for cross-team/irreversible decisions
- Bias towards trust

**Fast Feedback Mechanisms**:
- Review PRs within hours
- Regular prototype review sessions
- Rapid retrospectives after experiments

## Common Scenarios

### Unclear Requirements
- ❌ Low Agency: "I'll wait for PM to provide complete specs"
- ✅ High Agency: "I'll write my understanding and assumptions, share for validation"
- ✅ Action Bias: "I'll build a quick prototype and get feedback"

### Technical Debt
- ❌ Low Agency: "Management doesn't prioritize it, nothing I can do"
- ✅ High Agency: "I'll refactor code I'm working in as part of features"
- ✅ Action Bias: "Boy Scout Rule - leave code better than I found it"

### Cross-Team Dependency
- ❌ Low Agency: "I'm blocked, nothing I can do"
- ✅ High Agency: "I'll build a mock to continue parallel work"
- ✅ Action Bias: "I'll reach out to understand timeline and see if I can help"

### Learning New Technology
- ❌ Low Agency: "I need formal training first"
- ✅ High Agency: "I'll learn through documentation and tutorials this week"
- ✅ Action Bias: "I'll build a simple prototype to learn hands-on"

## Potential Pitfalls

### Over-Application
- **Lone Wolf Syndrome**: Acting independently when collaboration needed
- **Reckless Action**: Moving fast on irreversible decisions
- **Authority Overreach**: Making decisions outside your scope

**Solution**: Combine high agency with clear scope understanding (Locus of Control).

### Under-Application
- **Learned Helplessness**: Believing you can't influence outcomes
- **Analysis Paralysis**: Excessive planning on reversible decisions
- **Scope Confusion**: Unclear about what you can control

**Solution**: Start with small, low-risk actions; use 15-Minute Rule and Two-Way Door Principle.

## Measurement Indicators

### Individual High Agency
**Behavioral Signals**:
- ✅ Proactively solves problems without prompting
- ✅ Makes decisions within scope without excessive approvals
- ✅ Recovers quickly from setbacks
- ✅ Volunteers for challenges
- ✅ Takes ownership of outcomes

**Language Patterns**:
- ✅ "I tried X, learned Y, now doing Z"
- ✅ "I'll handle that" vs. "Someone should handle that"
- ✅ "Here's what I can do" vs. "Here's why it can't be done"
- ✅ "I'll start today" vs. "I need to wait for..."

### Team High Agency
**Cultural Signals**:
- Default response is "let's try" not "let's wait"
- Prototypes and experiments are common
- Failure discussed openly as learning
- Ideas flow from all levels

**Team Metrics**:
- Deployment frequency
- Cycle time (commit to production)
- Experiment velocity
- Mean time to recovery (MTTR)

## Quick Reference: The Amazon Two-Way Door Principle

**Type 2 Decisions (Two-Way Doors)** - MOST engineering decisions:
- ✅ Reversible or easy to undo
- ✅ Should be made quickly by individuals/small groups
- ✅ Accept some mistakes as learning
- ✅ Optimize for speed and iteration

**Type 1 Decisions (One-Way Doors)** - FEW engineering decisions:
- ⚠️ Irreversible or very difficult to reverse
- ⚠️ Require careful deliberation and broad consensus
- ⚠️ Merit extensive analysis
- ⚠️ Justify slower decision-making

**High Agency Insight**: Most engineers treat too many decisions as Type 1 when they're actually Type 2. High-agency engineers correctly identify Type 2 decisions and act quickly.
